No prior remote-sensing knowledge assumed.

## 2.1 Why radar and not a camera

A normal satellite camera (called **EO**, electro-optical) is useless for this job about half
the time. It cannot see at night, and it cannot see through cloud. Oil spills do not wait for
clear daylight.

**SAR — Synthetic Aperture Radar** — solves this. It is an *active* sensor: instead of waiting
for sunlight, the satellite fires its own microwave pulses at the sea and measures how much
energy bounces back. Microwaves pass straight through cloud, and the satellite brings its own
illumination, so SAR works at night, in cloud, in monsoon. That is why every operational oil
spill service on earth is built on SAR.

The image you get is not a photograph. It is a map of **backscatter** — how much radar energy
each patch of sea threw back at the satellite. Bright = a lot came back. Dark = very little.

### Why oil is dark

This is the one piece of physics you must be able to say out loud.

The sea surface is covered in tiny ripples, centimetres across, generated by wind. Radar at
Sentinel-1's wavelength (about 5.6 cm, called **C-band**) bounces off ripples of roughly its own
size very efficiently — this is called **Bragg scattering**. Rough sea, lots of ripples, lots of
energy back, bright image.

**Oil damps those ripples.** A film of oil increases the surface tension damping and flattens
the small-scale ripples out. No ripples, no Bragg scattering, almost nothing comes back to the
satellite. The oil appears as a **dark patch on a bright sea**.

Say it as one sentence: *"Wind makes ripples, ripples reflect radar, oil flattens the ripples,
so oil shows up as a dark hole in the radar image."*

### The wind window — an honest limitation to know

This mechanism only works in a band of wind speeds, roughly **3 to 10 m/s**:

- **Below ~3 m/s**: there are no ripples anywhere, so the whole sea is dark and oil is
  indistinguishable from calm water.
- **Above ~10 m/s**: the sea is so rough that the oil film gets broken up and mixed into the
  water column, and the surface roughens over the slick anyway.

So SAR oil detection has a built-in blind spot at both ends. Knowing this makes you sound like
you have read the literature.

### Look-alikes — the hardest real problem

Many things other than oil flatten the sea or otherwise look dark:

| Look-alike | Why it's dark |
|---|---|
| **Low-wind zones** | no ripples to reflect, same signature as oil |
| **Algal blooms / biogenic slicks** | natural surfactants from plankton damp ripples exactly like oil |
| **Rain cells** | rain damps short waves and scatters the signal |
| **Ship wakes** | turbulent, oily and surfactant-rich |
| **Upwelling and internal waves** | change surface roughness patterns |
| **Wind shadows near land** | sheltered water stays smooth |

This is *the* unsolved problem in the field and it is where a judge will push hardest. Our
current model has never been shown a labelled look-alike (the dataset contains none), so it is
untested against them. Say that plainly — see document 5.

## 2.2 Sentinel-1 specifically, since that is our data

| Thing | Value | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Operator | ESA (European Space Agency) | free and open data, no licence fee |
| Band | C-band, ~5.6 cm wavelength | the Bragg-scattering length that oil damps |
| Our satellites | Sentinel-1A (1,076 scenes), Sentinel-1B (124) | S1B failed in Dec 2021; S1C now flies in its place |
| Mode | **IW** — Interferometric Wide swath | the default over coasts, ~250 km wide strip |
| Pixel spacing | ~10 m for the standard IW product | one pixel ≈ a tennis court |
| Polarisations | **VV** and **VH** | see below |
| Revisit | roughly 6 days with two satellites | **not real time** — never claim real time |
| Our scenes | 2048 × 2048 pixels | ~20 × 20 km per scene at 10 m |

### VV and VH — the "two channels" our model takes

Radar can transmit and receive waves oriented vertically or horizontally. Sentinel-1 gives us
two combinations:

- **VV** — transmit **V**ertical, receive **V**ertical. This is *co-polarised*. Bragg scattering
  is strongest here, so VV shows the best contrast between rough sea and flat oil. **VV is the
  main channel for oil detection.**
- **VH** — transmit **V**ertical, receive **H**orizontal. This is *cross-polarised*. Much weaker
  signal, noisier, but it responds differently to some surfaces, which helps separate real oil
  from some look-alikes.

Think of them as two greyscale channels of the same scene, like R and G in an RGB image. That
is literally how our model treats them: `inChannels = 2`.

### The dB scale

Backscatter values span orders of magnitude, so they are converted to **decibels** — a
logarithmic scale. Our files are named `Sigma0_VV_db` and `Sigma0_VH_db`. "Sigma0" is the
calibrated backscatter coefficient; "db" means it has been log-scaled. Typical sea values are
negative numbers around −5 to −25 dB. Oil sits lower (darker) than the surrounding sea.

### Speckle

Because radar is *coherent*, waves returning from within a single pixel interfere with each
other and produce a grainy salt-and-pepper texture called **speckle**. It is not sensor noise
you can average away by taking a better picture — it is intrinsic to the imaging physics. It
must be filtered, and filtering it always costs some spatial detail. Our data has already been
speckle-filtered (see next section), and our classical baseline detector despeckles again
before thresholding.

### What has already been done to our files

Every image in `Oil/` carries the processing chain `Orb, NR, Cal, Spk, TC, dB` in its metadata.
Decoded:

| Step | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Orb** | precise orbit file applied — accurate satellite position |
| **NR** | thermal noise removal |
| **Cal** | radiometric calibration — raw counts → physical backscatter |
| **Spk** | speckle filtering |
| **TC** | terrain correction / geocoding — pixels placed on the earth |
| **dB** | converted to the decibel scale |

This is the standard ESA SNAP toolbox pre-processing chain. It matters because it means our
imagery is analysis-ready, and you can say so: *"the scenes are calibrated, speckle-filtered,
terrain-corrected Sigma0 in dB — we did not have to do the SAR pre-processing ourselves."*

## 2.3 Geospatial basics

**EPSG:4326** is the coordinate system our images use — plain latitude/longitude on the WGS84
ellipsoid, the same thing your phone's GPS reports. A **GeoTIFF** is a TIFF image with extra tags
saying where on earth each pixel sits: a *geotransform* giving the top-left corner coordinate
and the size of one pixel in degrees.

**Why area calculation is not just `pixels × constant`.** A degree of latitude is about 111 km
everywhere. A degree of *longitude* is 111 km at the equator but shrinks by `cos(latitude)` as
you go towards the poles — about 96 km at 30° N, about 64 km at 55° N. So a pixel that is
0.0001° × 0.0001° covers a different amount of ground depending on where it is. Multiply pixels
by a single flat constant and you get a systematically wrong area, and the error grows with
latitude.

Our geometry module integrates area row by row using the actual latitude of each row, on a
sphere. That is why the number on screen is defensible. It is also a very good 15-second answer
when a judge asks "how do you compute area?"

## 2.4 The AI part — segmentation

You know AI, so this section is short.

**The task is semantic segmentation**: classify *every pixel* as oil or not-oil. Input is a
2-channel image, output is a per-pixel probability map. Not classification (one label per
image), not detection (bounding boxes) — per-pixel masks, because we need the shape to compute
area and to seed drift particles.

**U-Net** is the standard architecture for this. It came out of biomedical image segmentation
(Ronneberger et al., 2015) and it is an encoder–decoder:

- The **encoder** downsamples, learning what things are while losing where they are.
- The **decoder** upsamples back to full resolution.
- **Skip connections** copy the high-resolution feature maps from each encoder level across to
  the matching decoder level, restoring the spatial precision the encoder threw away.

That last part is why U-Net wins at segmentation: you get semantic understanding *and* sharp
boundaries. Boundaries matter to us because area and perimeter are deliverables.

### The metrics, plainly

Let TP = pixels we correctly called oil, FP = pixels we wrongly called oil, FN = oil pixels we
missed.

| Metric | Formula | In words |
|---|---|---|
| **IoU** (Jaccard) | `TP / (TP + FP + FN)` | overlap ÷ combined area. The standard segmentation score. Harsh — punishes both kinds of error. |
| **Dice** (F1) | `2TP / (2TP + FP + FN)` | same idea, gentler. Always ≥ IoU, so never compare one team's Dice to another's IoU. |
| **Precision** | `TP / (TP + FP)` | of everything we called oil, how much really was. Low precision = false alarms. |
| **Recall** | `TP / (TP + FN)` | of all the real oil, how much we found. Low recall = missed spills. |
| **Accuracy** | correct ÷ total | **almost useless here.** If 1% of pixels are oil, calling everything "clean" scores 99%. Never lead with accuracy. |

**The precision/recall trade-off is an operational choice, not a bug.** Our model has recall
0.93 and precision 0.83 — it over-calls slightly. For disaster response that is the right
direction: missing a real spill is worse than sending an analyst to check a false one. Be ready
to say that; it shows you understand the deployment, not just the metric.

### Threshold

The model outputs a probability per pixel. To get a binary mask you pick a cutoff. Raise it and
precision goes up, recall goes down. It must be chosen on **validation** data and then applied
unchanged to **test** data — choosing it on test is cheating, and a sharp judge may ask. We
choose 0.8 on validation and report test at 0.8.

### Why patch metrics flatter every team's numbers

Our model is trained and scored on 128 × 128 pixel **patches** cut out of the big scenes. Those
patches are sampled around labelled oil. So the model is being examined on a region where oil is
about 18–38% of pixels — an exam where the answer is nearly always "yes, oil, right here."

A real scene is 2048 × 2048 and is 99%-plus open water. All the false alarms live out there in
the water the patch sampler never showed the model. That is why we also score **whole scenes**,
and why the whole-scene number is lower and is the honest one.

**Almost every team will quote a patch number without knowing this.** Knowing it, and
volunteering both numbers, is one of the strongest moves available to you.

## 2.5 Drift physics

### Lagrangian particle tracking

Do not try to model the slick as a continuous fluid. Instead: scatter thousands of imaginary
particles over the detected oil, and move each one independently, small time step by small time
step, by whatever the water and wind are doing at its own position:

```
for each time step:
    for each particle:
        u, v = current_velocity(particle.lon, particle.lat, t)
        u, v = u + windage × wind_u,  v + windage × wind_v
        u, v = u + random_turbulent_kick
        particle.move(u, v, dt)
```

This is called a **Lagrangian** model (follow the particles) as opposed to **Eulerian** (watch
fixed grid cells). It is what every operational spill model uses, because it handles a slick
breaking into fragments naturally.

### Hindcast versus forecast

- **Forecast** — integrate *forwards*. Where will the oil be in 24 hours? This is for the
  cleanup crew and the coastline at risk.
- **Hindcast** — integrate *backwards*, with the velocity field negated. Where was this oil 24
  hours ago? This is for the investigator, and it is NTRO's own word for it.

**A hindcast never gives you a point.** You start from a spread-out slick, uncertainty
accumulates every step, and the particle cloud fans out as you go back in time. What you get is
a **probability region** and a **time window** — "released somewhere in here, some time in this
24-hour span." That vagueness is honest and unavoidable, and it is exactly the search box you
hand to the AIS stage.

### Windage — why meteorological data is required

Surface oil does not just follow the current. Wind pushes it directly. The standard operational
rule of thumb is that oil moves at about **2–4% of the wind speed**, in roughly the wind
direction. We use **3%** (`windage_factor = 0.03`).

Do the arithmetic and you see why the PS demands meteorological data: a 6 m/s wind contributes
0.18 m/s of drift, which over 24 hours is about 15 km. Currents in many areas are a similar
magnitude. **Ignore wind and your origin estimate can be tens of kilometres wrong**, which means
you search the wrong water and shortlist the wrong ships.

### What we do not model — know these before a judge names them

- **Stokes drift** — waves impart a net forward drift on floating material beyond the mean
  current. Real, sometimes significant, not in our model.
- **Weathering** — real oil evaporates, emulsifies, disperses and sinks over hours to days.
  Our particles are conserved forever. This makes long hindcasts less trustworthy than short
  ones.
- **Spreading physics** — real slicks spread under gravity, viscosity and surface tension. We
  approximate all unresolved spreading with a turbulent random-walk term.

### Where real forcing data comes from

| Source | What | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **CMEMS** (Copernicus Marine Service) | global/regional ocean currents, reanalysis and forecast | free, EU; the `.nc` file in this repo is CMEMS |
| **INCOIS** (Hyderabad, Ministry of Earth Sciences) | Indian Ocean currents and forecasts | **India's own agency; they run operational oil-spill trajectory modelling.** Citing INCOIS is an Indian-relevance win with an NTRO audience |
| **ERA5** (ECMWF) | reanalysis winds | the standard free wind product |

These come as **NetCDF** (`.nc`) files — a self-describing gridded binary format for
multi-dimensional scientific arrays, indexed by latitude, longitude, depth and time. We have a
NetCDF reader in `services/common/spilltrace_common/`; the problem is our supplied file's dates
do not overlap our imagery's dates, so we fall back to synthetic forcing.

## 2.6 AIS — the ship tracking half

**AIS (Automatic Identification System)** is a VHF radio transponder that ships broadcast from
continuously — identity, position, speed, course — several times a minute. It exists for
collision avoidance. It is mandatory under SOLAS for essentially all large commercial vessels
and all passenger ships. Coastal stations and satellites listen, so historic archives exist.

Think **Flightradar24, for ships**.

### The fields, and which ones matter to us

| Field | Meaning | Why we care |
|---|---|---|
| **MMSI** | Maritime Mobile Service Identity — 9 digits, first 3 are the country code (MID) | the per-voyage identity key |
| **IMO number** | permanent 7-digit hull ID, survives resale and reflagging | the identity that cannot be laundered |
| **Call sign** | radio call sign | cross-reference |
| **BaseDateTime** | timestamp of the report | half of "spatio-temporal" |
| **LAT / LON** | position | the other half |
| **SOG** | Speed Over Ground, knots | a stop near the origin is a behavioural flag |
| **COG** | Course Over Ground, degrees true — the direction actually travelled | trajectory scoring |
| **Heading** | where the bow is pointing | differs from COG when wind or current pushes the hull sideways |
| **VesselType** | tanker, cargo, fishing, passenger… | a tanker can discharge oil in bulk; a ferry realistically cannot |
| **Status** | under way / at anchor / moored / restricted manoeuvrability | context for a stop |
| **Length / Width / Draft** | dimensions | plausible discharge volume |
| **TransceiverClass** | Class A (large ships, frequent reports) or Class B (small craft, sparse) | how much to trust sparse tracks |

Our synthetic feed carries its own richer internal fields (`mmsi, name, type, sogKn, cogDeg,
headingDeg, lon, lat, timeUtc` and more), but it **exports** in exactly the 17-column header
above — download it from `/api/cases/demo/ais.csv` and diff it against a real daily extract. The
same module also parses a real extract back in, so switching to a licensed feed is a file drop.
See document 3, §3.10 item 1.

### Behavioural anomalies — the PS asks for these by name

| Anomaly | Why it's interesting |
|---|---|
| **Slowing down or stopping** at sea, away from any port or anchorage | discharging takes time at low speed |
| **An AIS gap** — the transponder goes silent, "going dark" | the classic evasion signature |
| **A course deviation** then a return to the original track | a detour to somewhere |
| **Course across the drift axis** rather than along it | consistent with laying a trail |
| **Sparse or field-incomplete reporting** | may be degraded reception — or deliberate |

Our scorer implements stop/slow-down detection, envelope intersection, and cross-axis course.
Note the honesty requirement: an AIS gap can equally be a genuine equipment fault or a
reception hole. It raises a question; it does not answer one.

### Two caveats to volunteer before you are asked

- **AIS can be spoofed** — position and identity are self-reported and unauthenticated.
- **A vessel with AIS off is invisible to this method entirely.** SAR can see the *ship* as a
  bright metal target even when it is not transmitting, which is how you would catch a dark
  ship — a genuinely good answer to give, and a real future feature.

### The legal frame, in one paragraph

Operational oil discharge from ships is regulated internationally under **MARPOL Annex I**
(the oil annex of the IMO's MARPOL convention). Machinery-space bilge water may only be
discharged through approved separation equipment below a strict oil-content limit, and certain
sea areas are designated **Special Areas** with tighter rules. Deliberate tank washing or
bilge dumping at sea is an offence — which is exactly why attribution matters and why a spill
that "remains un-attributable" means nobody is penalised.

*Verify the specific numbers and the Special Area list before quoting them on stage.* Getting a
regulatory detail wrong in front of NTRO is worse than not mentioning it. "MARPOL Annex I
governs this and deliberate discharge is an offence" is safe; precise ppm limits are not, unless
you have checked.

## 2.7 Why "spatio-temporal" is the whole trick

The phrase appears in the PS's expected solution and it names the core idea. Neither space nor
time alone is enough:

- **Space only** — "which ships passed through this patch of sea?" Dozens, over a week. Useless.
- **Time only** — "which ships were sailing at 02:00 on 11 March?" Thousands. Useless.
- **Both, intersected** — "which ships were inside *this* region during *this* 24-hour window?"
  A handful. That is a shortlist.

Our hindcast produces exactly that box: a region and a window. Everything before it in the
pipeline exists to compute that box, and everything after it exists to rank what falls inside.
If you can explain that one paragraph, you can explain the project.

## 2.9 The minimum you must be able to say

If you learn nothing else, learn these seven. Each should come out in one or two sentences,
unhesitatingly.

1. **Why radar** — sees at night and through cloud; a camera cannot.
2. **Why oil is dark** — wind makes ripples, ripples reflect radar, oil flattens the ripples.
3. **What VV and VH are** — two radar polarisations, two input channels; VV carries most of the
   oil signal.
4. **What IoU is** — overlap divided by combined area, and why accuracy is meaningless when oil
   is 1% of pixels.
5. **Why patch metrics flatter and whole-scene metrics are honest** — the patch sampler only
   looks where the oil already is.
6. **What a hindcast is and why it gives a region, not a point** — uncertainty grows every step
   backwards.
7. **Why you need both space and time** — either alone gives you thousands of ships; the
   intersection gives you a shortlist.

Everything else you can look up in this file between rounds.
